# Pokhara Job Trends — Talk Brief

**Source:** Adarsha Samaj classifieds, 10 issues, 2013–2026 · 93 distinct ads, 196 role records
**Prepared:** 2026-09-30

---

## The one-sentence frame

**A newspaper archive is a time machine: I found a way to read fourteen years of Pokhara's job ads at once, and the first thing it revealed was a national policy decision disappearing from a local paper's pages.**

That framing is honest (you did build the reader), it's the strongest thing in the data, and it doesn't require you to overclaim the numbers.

---

## The three movements

### 1. This is a service economy, and print shows it plainly

Of 93 ads, **75% fall in four sectors** — admin/office (26%), teaching (24%), hospitality (19%), security (6%). There is essentially **no industrial or trades hiring** in the print classifieds beyond one furniture-industry helper ad and a plumber.

Pokhara's printed labour market is tourism, education and office work. If you want a job in this city and you read the newspaper, you are competing for service-sector roles.

### 2. Foreign-employment advertising vanished — and you can date it

| Year | Ads | Foreign/manpower ads | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2013 | 31 | 11 | **35%** |
| 2016 | 32 | 0 | 0% |
| 2019 | 11 | 1 | 9% |
| 2022 | 6 | 0 | 0% |
| 2026 | 13 | 0 | 0% |

**This is your best story.** A third of all job advertising in this newspaper in 2013 was about leaving Nepal. By 2016 there was none.

That aligns with Nepal's 2017 ban on citizen labour departures to the Gulf, which shut the recruitment channel. The destinations named in those ads — UAE/Dubai (8), Malaysia (4), Qatar (4) — are exactly the Gulf states the ban targeted.

**The insight worth saying out loud:** a national policy decision, passed in Kathmandu, is *legible in the classified pages of a regional paper in Pokhara.* Policy leaves a paper trail. Nobody announced this. You can only see it by reading the ads across time.

**Be ready for this challenge:** "your 2016 figure is zero, but the ban was 2017." True — and you should concede it. With one issue in 2016 and one in 2019, the *exact year* isn't established; what's established is that a category that was a third of the market vanished. Say the collapse is real and the precise timing needs more data.

### 3. Degrees are not what this market asks for

- **Only 20% of ads state any education requirement.**
- Where they do, **+2 (Higher Secondary) is the usual ceiling**; SLC (Secondary) is the common floor.
- Bachelor-level requirements appear only in the better-paid professional and training roles.

**The tension worth naming:** Pokhara has 25+ colleges and produces thousands of graduates a year. The print labour market's most common requirement is "SLC passed" or "experienced." The credential supply and the credential demand are not the same curve.

Combine this with the application channel: **72% of ads cite a mobile number.** You apply by phone call, not by form.

---

## Two supporting facts

**Pay is enormously dispersed, and mostly hidden.** Only 47% of ads state any salary, and many decline to give one — *"as per organisation rules"*, *"to be decided by mutual discussion"*, *"attractive."* Where figures exist, the 2013 spread runs **Rs 4,500 (general staff) to Rs 70,000 (security guard) — about 15×**. Guarding paid far above office and general staff.

**Employers often can't describe the job.** 23% of ads are vague generic listings — `कर्मचारी` (staff), `साधारण कर्मचारी` (general staff) — with no role specified. That's a thin market where employers struggle to articulate what they need.

The one standout: **Bhasker Memorial School** published a full salary band for all 7 posts plus written-exam and interview dates. Transparency like that is rare.

## One extra thread, if you want it

**Hotel Silent Peak advertised 5 times** — more than any other employer, once as a multi-role hotel staff ad and across consecutive issues. Two of the four repeat advertisers were overseas-job agencies. Repeat advertising is a reasonable proxy for *ongoing* recruitment, which matters for anyone trying to track live openings rather than historical ones.

---

## What you must not claim

Say these plainly if asked, and you'll sound more credible, not less:

- **93 ads is not a market.** It's a pilot. Treat every percentage as a sample description, never a market share.
- **68% of the sample is 2013–2016.** The current market is 13 ads from 2026. The past is far better described than the present.
- **The 10 issues were chosen to test old fonts and layouts, not sampled at random.** So sector shares aren't representative by design.
- **One issue per year for 2013–2022** (five issues in 2026). A single issue cannot represent a year.
- **2018 is missing from the archive** — 89 of 356 issues survived. Any trend crossing 2018 has a permanent hole.
- **6% of extracted records were course catalogues, not jobs**, and were excluded. (13 of 218.)
- **Cross-era salaries aren't comparable as printed** — no inflation adjustment.

The honest version of your closing line: *"This is 93 advertisements, and it is not enough to describe a city. But it is enough to show that a question can be asked of fourteen years of print that nobody thought to ask — and that the answer was hiding in plain sight."*

## Likely questions

**"How do you know this reflects the real job market?"**
It doesn't claim to. It's what the *newspaper* advertised. Many Pokhara jobs are now found on Merojob, LinkedIn and Facebook — so this is the print-demand shadow of the market, which is itself a limitation worth naming.

**"Isn't the decline in ads just people moving online?"**
Very likely part of it. Ad density fell from ~4 ads/page (2013–16) to 0.7 (2026) — but modern issues are also 4 pages versus 6–8 in older years, and I hand-checked two 2026 pages that triage marked as having no jobs; both were correct. So it isn't purely an extraction failure, but I can't yet separate "fewer print ads" from "ads moved online."

**"Isn't it a small sample?"**
Yes. One issue per year for most years. That's why the foreign-employment collapse is the finding I lead with: it's large, it's directionally clear, and it has an external explanation I can point to.

**"What would make it solid?"**
Extracting the remaining 23,595 pages. The archive is already downloaded and parsed; only the reading is missing.
